Seasonal

Hay fever & healing with homeopathy

From Charles Blackley’s 19th-century experiments to the remedies homeopaths consider today — and Vinita’s own published case series.

Dr Vinita Pandey PhD, LCHE, RSHom
14 October 2020 · 8 min read

Sneezing bouts, a runny or blocked nose, itchy, red or watery eyes, an itchy throat and malaise. Many people suffer these symptoms as spring arrives. Rather than enjoying the warm sun and the blooming flowers, they take refuge indoors to avoid triggers.

Different plants release pollen at different times of year, from spring right through to autumn, prolonging the season for many. In spring and summer grass pollen is the major culprit; in autumn, mould and fungal spores affect many people.

A little history

Hay fever as a recognised condition can be traced to the early 19th century. In 1871 Charles Blackley, a physician, demonstrated after ten years of research that pollen causes its symptoms. He reproduced them in his laboratory by placing a small amount of grass pollen in the noses of his patients, and invented the skin test in its simplest form: scratching pollen onto the forearm, where only people with hay fever developed a red wheal.

Blackley also noticed that the “farming classes” rarely suffered — either, he thought, because of continued exposure, or because they lacked “the predisposition caused by academic toil”.

What is hay fever?

Hay fever is a common allergic condition affecting up to one in five people at some point in their lives. An allergy is an exaggerated immune reaction to something normally harmless. The immune system treats the allergen — here, pollen — as dangerous and produces a special antibody (IgE). This leads other cells to release chemicals, including histamine, that cause the symptoms; that is why antihistamines help. Steroid nasal sprays are used when antihistamines are not enough.

For more severe allergies, immunotherapy involves regular, small doses of the allergen over months or years, gradually desensitising the body. The idea of using small doses of what causes a problem is also the basis of homeopathy.

My own research

In 2016 I published a small case series from my Wimbledon clinic in the journal Homeopathy, following eight patients with hay fever (read the paper). It was a modest first step: larger, controlled studies would be needed before drawing firm conclusions.

Ways of prescribing

Isopathic prescribing uses the substance a person has been shown to be allergic to (for example house dust mite or mixed pollens), prepared as a homeopathic potency.

Therapeutic prescribing focuses on the acute symptoms. Remedy pictures homeopaths often consider include:

Allium cepa — made from red onion — think of cutting a raw onion. Bland, streaming eyes but a runny nose that irritates the nostrils, with violent sneezing; worse in a warm room, better in cool open air.

Euphrasia — ‘eyebright’, considered when the eyes are most affected, with a gritty, sandy feeling and eyes stuck together in the morning.

Sabadilla — constant sneezing with an itchy, runny nose and watery eyes.

Wyethia — intense itching in the nose, ears and, strikingly, the roof of the mouth, with an urge to rub the palate with the tongue.

Arundo — unusual itching, especially inside the ears, with a runny nose and loss of the sense of smell.

Apis — made from the honey bee: puffy, swollen, stinging eyelids that feel better for a cold compress.

Constitutional prescribing looks at the whole person. Remedies I often consider include:

Arsenicum album — marked restlessness, mental and physical, with anxiety around midnight to 3am.

Natrum muriaticum — discharges like raw egg white, violent sneezing in the mornings, and sometimes headaches made worse by the sun or by emotional sensitivity.

Silicea — people with low resistance who catch frequent colds and sore throats, with thick yellow-green catarrh.

I have mentioned only a few remedies. A detailed consultation with a qualified homeopath is the way to find the one that suits you (registered homeopaths can be found through the Society of Homeopaths).

Traditional herbal approaches

These herbs have long traditions of use. Please check with a pharmacist or your GP before taking any herbal product, especially if you are pregnant, breastfeeding or taking other medicines.

Butterbur — its broad leaves were once used to wrap butter. It has a long history of traditional use, and a study in the British Medical Journal compared butterbur tablets with an antihistamine for hay fever over two weeks. Only use licensed products labelled free of pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PA-free).

Turmeric — prized in Ayurvedic and Chinese medicine. A pleasant way to enjoy it is ‘turmeric honey’: mix equal amounts of turmeric and honey and take a teaspoon now and then.

Stinging nettle (Urtica dioica) — used in both herbal medicine and homeopathy. Laboratory research has looked at how nettle extract affects receptors and enzymes involved in allergy. A comforting way to take it is as a tea: simmer a teaspoon of dried nettle leaves in boiling water for a few minutes and add honey to taste.

Please read: this article is general information from Vinita’s practice, not personal medical advice. Homeopathy is complementary to — not a replacement for — medical care. Don’t self-prescribe for persistent or worrying symptoms, and never stop prescribed medicine without your doctor’s advice. Wheezing, breathlessness or chest tightness need medical assessment — keep using any inhaler your doctor has prescribed.
